# Findings

What this benchmark has established, what it falsified, and what it learned
about measuring itself. `RESULTS.md` holds the numbers and is generated by
`--summarise`; this file holds the reasoning, which a table cannot carry.

`prime` / `test-0.1`, 280 queries, k=20, metrics from the official
`stark_qa` evaluator. Campaign of 2026-08-19/21.

**This file was reorganised on 2026-08-21 and had until then grown by pure
accretion** — 228 lines to 619, one appended section per session, twenty
lines ever deleted. The cost of that was not length, it was staleness:
later sections silently answered questions earlier ones still described as
open, and superseded numbers stayed in place because nothing removed them.
It is now organised by what a reader needs rather than by when we learned
it. Where a belief changed, both the belief and what killed it are kept —
those are Part II and they are the most useful part of this document.

# Part 0 — How to read a number here

**Nothing below is comparable to anything else until you have checked
these three things.**

## The retrieval basis

Retrieval became index-backed on 2026-08-21. Every live tenant has a
partial HNSW index and the database runs `hnsw.ef_search = 800`. Every
report records `retrieval_is_exact` and `hnsw_ef_search`, and `RESULTS.md`
carries a `retrieval` column reading `exact`, `hnsw/ef=N`, or `--` for
reports written before the provenance block existed.

An approximate number and an exact one are otherwise indistinguishable on
disk, and the gap is not small — see Part III for what `ef_search = 200`
cost. **Check the column before comparing two rows.**

`vss-control` is deliberately the one unindexed arm, because its exactness
is a standing reproducibility check.

## The noise floors

| arms | floor | source |
|---|---|---|
| LLM (rerank, zero_shot, deep) | ~0.001 mrr; hit@5 ~0.011 | `B-LLM-RUN-NOISE-1` |
| retrieval-only (dense, hybrid) | ~0.0002 mrr | `B-QUERY-EMBED-NONDETERMINISM-1` |
| lexical | none measured | no live embedding call |

A difference below the floor is not a result. Two arms in Part I.2 land
within 0.0002 of each other and are reported as indistinguishable rather
than as a ranking, which is the point of having the floors written down.

## What the numbers are numbers *for*

Every local embedding figure is for a **Q8_0 GGUF served by llama.cpp
with `--pooling last`**, not for the model as published. Every LLM figure
is for a specific quantisation on a specific serving stack. A score is a
property of the served artefact.

# Part I — What the campaign established

## I.1 Corpus construction beats architecture, and it lands on the lexical channel

### Relational text in the indexed document

Two arms, same model (`qwen3-embedding-0.6b`), same ~1.0 chunks/node,
differing only in whether the indexed document carries a `- relations:`
block naming the node's neighbours. Both on the current indexed basis:

| agent | `qwen-wholedoc` (no relations) | `qwen-rel-whole` (relations) | change |
|---|---|---|---|
| dense | 0.16684 | 0.18269 | +0.016 |
| lexical | 0.20479 | 0.24913 | **+0.044 (+22%)** |
| hybrid | 0.19207 | 0.27711 | **+0.085 (+44%)** |

**The mechanism was not predicted.** PRIME's queries name related entities
verbatim — *"a drug that targets X and is indicated for Y"*. Those names
appear in the answer's own document **only** in the relations version.
BM25 matches them directly; a single dense vector compresses them away. So
nearly the whole gain arrives through the lexical channel.

**A retrodiction it explains.** Across the older arms, `lexical` beat
`dense` on 5 of 9 configs, which read as noise at the time. It was this
effect showing through weakly on corpora that lacked the relational text.

**What follows for the architecture.** A knowledge graph's edges are worth
more as *text in the index* than as a traversal at query time, at least on
this benchmark, and worth most to the retriever that can match a name
exactly. That is a result about how to use a graph, not about how good the
graph is.

### Chunking that corpus, which we predicted would not help

`qwen-rel-sliding1k` against `qwen-rel-whole`: same model, same corpus,
same tenant scheme, `sliding-1000-500` versus `whole-document`, 4.250
chunks/node against 1.002.

| agent | whole-document | sliding-1000-500 | change |
|---|---|---|---|
| dense | 0.18269 | **0.25319** | **+0.071** |
| lexical | 0.24913 | **0.32131** | **+0.072 (+29%)** |
| hybrid | 0.27711 | **0.34675** | **+0.070 (+25%)** |

**The config predicted the lexical half would not move.** Its own comment,
written before the run: *"Lexical may DEGRADE, or not move. BM25 already
sees every term in the document whether it is one chunk or four."*

The premise was right and the conclusion was wrong. BM25 does see every
term either way — but **length normalisation is the mechanism**, not a
detail. A term matched inside a 12,000-character document is heavily
discounted; the same term in a 1,000-character chunk is not, and
`aggregation: max` lets the node take its best chunk. Splitting the 280
queries by gold-document length:

| gold document length | mean change in reciprocal rank |
|---|---|
| shortest third (median 959 ch) | +0.061 |
| middle third (median 3,846 ch) | +0.055 |
| **longest third (median 14,326 ch)** | **+0.101** |

Roughly 1.7x larger where the gold document is longest, which is what
length normalisation predicts — though the shortest third gains
substantially too, so treat this as consistent with the mechanism rather
than as isolating it.

**The config's other prediction did hold, and was the more interesting
half**: dense improves too, because a 1000-character window isolates the
relations block into its own chunk instead of diluting it across one
whole-document vector. +0.071 on dense is the largest single-change gain
that channel has seen here — against +0.016 for adding the relational text
itself.

**Why the earlier chunking sweep found nothing.** It ran on `prime`, where
documents carry no relations block: median 103 characters against
`prime-rel`'s 357, with a far shorter tail. Chunking cannot rescue
documents from a length penalty they were never long enough to incur. The
sweep answered its question for the corpus it ran on, and that corpus was
the one where chunking had least to do.

### Where this leaves us against published work

`qwen-rel-sliding1k` hybrid at **0.34675 with no LLM** is above every
retriever published on this split (checked 2026-08-21 against the official
leaderboard and Table 6 of the paper): GritLM-7b 0.2499, ada-002 0.2350,
multi-ada-002 0.2370, BM25 0.2168.

**The comparison is worth making only because our embedder is worse than
theirs.** On the dense channel `qwen3-embedding-0.6b` scores 0.16684
against ada-002's 0.23057 on the same corpus shape — a 0.064 deficit. The
margin therefore comes from corpus construction and channel fusion, not
from model quality, and cannot be dismissed as "newer model". The rerank
comparison against the paper's `gpt-4-turbo` reranker carries no such
argument and should not be made.

## I.2 Reranking: the ceiling, and what selection is worth

### The fetch ceiling was binding, and lifting it worked

`rerank` fetches exactly `k`, so its returned set is `hybrid`'s **by
construction** and `recall@20` cannot move. A pure ordering experiment,
and a good one — 0.28214 → 0.41948 from reordering alone. But every gold
answer hybrid ranked 21st or worse was permanently unreachable.

| | fetch=20 | fetch=40 | change |
|---|---|---|---|
| mrr | 0.41948 | **0.46323** | +10.4% |
| hit@1 | 0.35357 | **0.40000** | +13.1% |
| **recall@20** | 0.46508 | **0.53693** | **+15.4%** |
| seconds | 5,162 | 10,079 | +95% |

**recall@20 moving is the whole result** — it was frozen at hybrid's
0.46508, and the reranker promoted enough real answers out of ranks 21–40
to add 15.4% more gold. On 69.2% of queries the returned set differs from
hybrid's, so the wider window is used heavily rather than decoratively.

The predicted downside is real and was outvoted, not absent: a wider fetch
lets the model demote a marginal hit off the end as well as promote one
up, and a 4-query probe had shown exactly that. Demotions still happen;
they are outnumbered. At `fetch=60` the balance could invert.

*(Both figures predate the index. The `ef=800` control re-run is in
flight; until it lands, do not compare them against arms on the current
basis.)*

### Selection is the lever, not volume

`rerank40title` sends each candidate as `name (type)` — ~37 characters
against a full document's 3,415. The arms below add **eight neighbour
names**, identical in count, shape and token cost, differing only in
*which* eight. The kept names differ on 48.2% of real candidates.

| passages shown to the reranker | mrr | hit@1 |
|---|---|---|
| none (`hybrid`, no reranking) | 0.28214 | 0.1679 |
| title only | 0.33975 | 0.2536 |
| **+8 relations, document order** | **0.31010** | 0.2071 |
| **+8 relations, BM25-ranked** | **0.39343** | 0.3071 |

**Arbitrary relational text is worse than none.** Eight names chosen by
document order cost **0.030 mrr** against showing no relations at all.
The same number chosen by BM25 gains 0.054 over titles. The swing
attributable to selection alone is **+0.083 mrr**, ~80x the LLM noise
floor.

So "more context helps the reranker" is false here. Names of entities
unrelated to the query are noise the model spends attention on.

**This does not contradict I.1.** There, whole relation blocks reach the
*retriever*, where BM25 selects at retrieval time and nothing has to
choose. On a lean encoding the choosing is explicit, and doing it badly is
worse than not doing it. The two agree on the mechanism — neighbour names
matter, lexically — and differ on what carries it.

### Which selector, answered

The obvious next move was a better selector, and the obvious candidate was
an embedding. All on `qwen-rel-whole`, `gemma-4-26b-qat`, `ef=800`:

| selector | mrr | hit@1 | recall@20 |
|---|---|---|---|
| `matrix` (three averaged score axes) | **0.41771** | 0.34643 | 0.52250 |
| `ranked` at fetch=80 | 0.40486 | 0.32500 | 0.51128 |
| `ranked` (BM25) at fetch=40 | 0.39343 | 0.30714 | 0.50672 |
| `dense` (embedding) | 0.37999 | 0.28929 | 0.49830 |
| `hybrid` (embedding + BM25) | 0.37984 | 0.28571 | 0.51491 |

**An embedding selector does not beat BM25**, and the two embedding
variants are within 0.0002 of each other — inside the noise floor, so not
distinguishable from one another either. The cheap selector wins.

**Averaging three score axes is the best lean encoding found**, +0.024
over the best single selector, well outside the floor.

**Widening to 80 candidates buys 0.011 for double the LLM calls** — the
recall curve has largely flattened past 40.

Every lean arm remains below `rerank40` on full documents. Whatever they
save in tokens they lose in ranking quality, and no selector tried has
closed the gap.

## I.3 Whether a retrieval-optimal chunker reranks well is UNRESOLVED

`qwen-rel-sliding1k` retrieves more than `qwen-rel-whole` — recall@20
0.50420 against 0.46432, the highest in the project — and reranking can
only reorder what retrieval found, so it ought to rerank at least as well.

**Three attempts to measure this produced three different answers, and
none of them is trustworthy.** The sequence is worth recording because
every step looked like a result at the time:

1. `qwen-rel-sliding1k` + `rerank40` scored 0.41341 against a standing
   0.46323 — reported as "retrieval and reranking want different things
   from a chunker", a 0.050 effect.
2. The baseline turned out to be a **different reranker model**.
   `DEFAULT_CHAT_MODEL` is `qwen3.8-27b-64k-txt`; the old run recorded no
   `chat_model` because nobody passed `--chat-model`, while every arm run
   since carries `gemma-4-26b-qat`. The comparison was cross-model and
   read as cross-corpus. A same-model control put the gap at 0.0054 with
   hit@1 inverted — no effect worth naming.
3. That control was itself **degraded**: 8.2% of its queries got `502 Bad
   Gateway` from the chat peer, fell back to retrieval order, and were
   never ranked. A `qwen3.8` arm run alongside it failed on *every* query
   the same way and scored 0.29188 — which reads exactly like a plausible
   architecture result and is nothing of the kind.

What stands: the two corpora have not been compared with one reranker on
one basis with all calls succeeding. Everything said about it so far was
an artefact. The clean run is queued.

**What this cost, and the fix it produced.** Attempt 3 is why
`agent_warnings` now exists in every report and why a run with any agent
warning exits non-zero (Part III). Both degraded runs logged
`rerank: extract failed` exactly as designed, and `run_queries` logged
`0 empty` — because retrieval order is not empty. Nothing read the log.

## I.4 The agentic arms disappointed

Scored only on the Nemotron corpora, which no longer exist, so these
cannot be reproduced. Their ingests carried all 8,100,498 edges, so
`deep`'s `neighbors` tool was live and `B-DEEP-EDGES-1` was satisfied.

| corpus | dense | hybrid | zero_shot | deep |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `native-wholedoc` | 0.2163 | 0.2187 | 0.2139 | 0.1851 |
| `native-sliding1k` | 0.2125 | 0.2211 | 0.2199 | 0.2015 |
| `redstring-native` | 0.1845 | 0.1985 | 0.1998 | — |

`zero_shot` costs one LLM call per query and matches or slightly trails
`hybrid`, which costs none. `deep` costs ~7.46 calls per query against a
cap of 8 — so most queries ran to exhaustion — and is the **worst** arm on
both corpora where it ran. Reranking, which is not agentic, beats
everything here by a wide margin (`native-wholedoc` `rerank` 0.3408).

The open question is not "do they work" but "do they work where retrieval
is strong and the graph has relational text to walk". That needs
`--ingest-edges` on a live tenant; the qwen arms have none.

## I.5 `boundary-preference` is an outlier, on three models

redstring's own default chunker came **last on dense retrieval** on
Nemotron, and again on qwen's mini set. `sliding-1000-500`, at more than
double the granularity, beat it both times, and the two obvious
explanations — lost content, chunk length — were ruled out earlier.

Three models agreeing points at the chunker rather than at an interaction
with one embedding model. This is the most concrete thing this benchmark
has found *about redstring*, and it deserves a passage-level diagnostic
rather than another arm.

# Part II — What this project believed and was wrong about

The most useful section here. Each of these drove real decisions before it
was killed.

## II.8 Query decomposition buys nothing on a relational corpus -- the corpus already did it

**Predicted before the run, in the module docstring**: `decompose` pools
100 candidates across the original query plus up to four sub-queries, so
unlike every `rerank*` arm it can surface a candidate a single `hybrid`
search never ranked. The stated test was recall@20 against
`rerank40titlerelranked`'s **0.50672**.

**Measured**: 0.47945. Below it, and barely above `hybrid`'s own 0.46821.

| arm | mrr | hit@1 | recall@20 | LLM calls | wall |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `hybrid` | 0.28156 | 0.1679 | 0.46821 | 0 | 65s |
| `decompose` v1, RRF fusion, pool 40 | 0.37127 | 0.3000 | 0.47843 | 2 | 912s |
| `decompose` v2, LLM union, pool 100 | 0.37947 | 0.3250 | 0.47945 | 2 | 1403s |
| `rerank40titlerelranked` | 0.39343 | 0.3071 | 0.50672 | 1 | -- |
| `rerank40titlerelmatrix` | 0.41344 | 0.3357 | 0.52786 | 1 | 919s |

**The number that settles it is the one that did not move.** Widening from
a 40-candidate fused pool to a 100-candidate union across five searches --
2.5x the pool, five retrievals instead of one -- bought **+0.001
recall@20**. Whatever the sub-queries retrieve, `hybrid` on the full query
had already found.

**Why, and it follows from this project's headline rather than
contradicting it.** The relational corpus solves the conjunction at *index*
time. A `- relations:` block puts every neighbour's name in the node's own
document, so BM25 matches "a drug that targets X and is indicated for Y"
against a document containing the literal strings X and Y. That is the
+22% lexical gain §I.1 records. Decomposition attacks the same weakness at
*query* time, and arrives to find it already fixed.

The prediction was built on the dense channel's failure to hold
conjunctions (+2% against lexical's +22%). That asymmetry is real, but it
describes the *dense* channel, and `hybrid` does not depend on the dense
channel for this. Reasoning from one channel's weakness to the fused
retriever's was the error.

**What the rewrite did establish.** The first version fused with RRF,
promoting a candidate for being found by several searches -- including
tangential ones. Replacing that with an LLM union that sees which searches
found each candidate and at what rank gained **+0.008 mrr and +0.025
hit@1**. So arithmetic fusion was the wrong step to automate; that is a
real result about how to combine multi-query retrieval, on an arm that
loses anyway.

**Where decomposition might still pay**, untested here: a corpus WITHOUT
relational text, where the conjunction is not pre-solved. Every `prime`
(non-`rel`) arm is such a corpus. That is the honest next experiment, and
it inverts the usual direction -- the technique should help *more* on the
weaker corpus.

## II.1 "ada-002 is reading a richer corpus"

`qwen-wholedoc` dense against `vss-control` dense was read as a corpus
difference — the theory being that STaRK's precomputed vectors are over
`add_rel=True` documents while local arms embedded `add_rel=False` text.

**Wrong.** Giving qwen the relational text moved dense by 0.016, leaving
the gap essentially intact. On the dense channel ada-002 is simply better
here than `qwen3-embedding-0.6b`.

It cost a day of endpoint time — and the arm built to test it produced the
project's best result anyway, for a mechanism nobody predicted. That is
the case for building the cell rather than arguing about it.

## II.2 "More context helps the reranker"

False, and expensively so: eight *arbitrarily chosen* neighbour names
score 0.030 **below** showing no relations at all (I.2).

## II.3 "A better selector means an embedding selector"

The argument was explicit: relations moved lexical +22% and dense +2%, so
a dense selector looked like dead weight; then I.2's selection result
suggested a *better* selector was the highest-value lever, and an
embedding-plus-BM25 hybrid was worth its port addition.

**Both embedding selectors lose to plain BM25** (I.2), and lose to each
other by less than the noise floor. Selection is the lever; a more
expensive selector is not.

## II.4 "Retrieval-only arms are reproducible"

This file and `CLAUDE.md` both said run-to-run noise was an LLM property
and retrieval-only arms were unaffected.

Two runs of `qwen-rel-sliding1k` dense — same code, same rows, no index in
either — disagreed on **103 of 280 queries**, moving mrr by 0.000054 and
recall@20 by 0.000397.

The cause is upstream of the database: the same text embedded twice
against the endpoint, alone, back to back, returns **different vectors**
(max component delta 3.3e-03, cosine 0.99981). Calls 2, 3 and 4 agree with
each other and differ from call 1, so it is the first call after idle
rather than per-call jitter. The mechanism is not pinned down.

The noise source was never "an LLM" — it is a batched inference server,
and the query embedder is one of those too. `vss-control` reproducing
`0.23057383129905376` to every digit is consistent with this rather than a
counter-example: its vectors are precomputed, so it makes no live
embedding call. That is also why it is the one arm left unindexed.

The cheap fix, unimplemented: chunk vectors are cached in
`kg_embedding_cache`; query vectors are not, and are re-embedded every
run.

## II.5 "A recall knob can be swept on a convenient corpus"

`hnsw.ef_search = 200` was chosen from a sweep on `qwen-rel-whole`
(129,656 rows), where it costs 0.0040 mrr against exact. On
`qwen-rel-sliding1k` (549,697 rows) the same setting costs **0.0110** —
2.8x more, and larger than the entire measured effect of several changes
in Part I.

The campaign had standardised on 200 and started the LLM arms — which
consume retrieval output and would have inherited the loss — before the
large corpus was measured. They were stopped and restarted.

**Sweep a recall knob on the largest corpus you have, not the fastest
one.**

## II.6 "Higher recall reranks better" — then "worse" — then neither

Mine, from 2026-08-21, and wrong three times in a row (I.3): a 0.050
effect that was a model confound, then a 0.0054 non-effect measured on a
run where 8.2% of queries silently never reached the model.

The lesson is not "be careful". It is that **each correction felt like
the end of the investigation**. The model confound was a satisfying
explanation, and it was also wrong to stop there. What broke the chain
was checking `llm_calls_per_query` against 1.0 — a number that was in
every report the whole time and that nothing was reading.

## II.7 "Adding an index makes retrieval faster"

It made it **3.1x slower** (Part III).

# Part III — How to measure this system

Every entry cost real time, and each is a trap that will be walked into
again by anyone who does not know it.

## Verify an index with `idx_scan`, never with your own `EXPLAIN`

redstring's `PostgresChunkStore` ranked by
`1 - (embedding <=> $2) / 2 DESC`, which is the same order as
`embedding <=> $2 ASC` but is **not a form any pgvector opclass can
serve**. Three partial HNSW indexes were built against it — 5.7GB, ~9
minutes — and all three finished at **`idx_scan = 0`**. Not one query used
them. Dense retrieval over the 549,697-row tenant went from 165.8s to
**519.1s**, a 3.1x slowdown, because 5.7GB of unread index evicted a
2.25GB table from the page cache.

`EXPLAIN` of `ORDER BY embedding <=> $1 LIMIT 20` shows a beautiful index
scan and describes **a query this codebase never issues**. It survived an
hour that way. The adapter's real SQL is a CTE with a rescaled score, a
`min_score` filter and a join, and plans as a sequential scan.

```sql
select indexrelname, idx_scan from pg_stat_user_indexes
 where indexrelname like '%hnsw%';
```

The counter cannot be argued with. It went 0 → 280 for a 280-query run the
moment the ordering was fixed (redstring PR #71).

## The recall/speed trade, measured rather than assumed

`qwen-rel-sliding1k`, 280 queries, `--query-concurrency 4`:

| | dense wall | hybrid wall | dense mrr Δ vs exact |
|---|---|---|---|
| exact | 165.8s | 372.3s | — |
| ef=200 | 4.6s | 246.9s | −0.0110 |
| ef=400 | 7.3s | 180.1s | −0.0028 |
| ef=800 | 5.9s | 183.9s | +0.0003 |

Neither wall column is monotonic in `ef_search`, and **that is the finding
rather than an anomaly**: page-cache state is larger than the effect.
Hybrid is *faster* at the wider setting because its cost is BM25 over the
terms table, which `ef_search` does not touch.

So the quality/speed trade at this corpus size is 0.011 mrr against about
one second per run. An earlier version of this section claimed "~30s at
200", which was **wrong** — carried over from a cold-cache run of a
different arm without checking. Quote the table.

## Only a window measures a rate, and the window must outlast the variance

A healthy ingest was diagnosed as hung on four signals that all looked
damning and were all misreads:

| signal | why it was wrong |
|---|---|
| log had stopped | `ingest done` prints *before* the 8.1M-edge phase, which logged nothing |
| chunk count flat over 30s | chunks commit in `CHUNK_BATCH = 1000` steps; ~6 minutes apart on long documents |
| client CPU unchanged over 20s | an I/O-bound client accrues under a second in 20s |
| `/slots` showed `processed: 0` | that is what a slot reports the instant it picks up a task |

What settled it: `id_task` climbing across three samples, and a 3-minute
window showing +5,010 chunks.

But "use a window" is not sufficient advice. One 25-query step of a rerank
run timed **8.8 s/query**; the whole-run average was **35.9**. The short
window was 4x optimistic and was believed *because* it was a window. A
100-query window agreed with the whole-run average to within 2%.
`checkpoint_every = 25` makes the predictions file a step function for the
same reason `CHUNK_BATCH` does — the same defect in a different unit,
walked into anyway.

## Extrapolate by characters, not documents — and even that has a caveat

A `--limit 3000` calibration implied a 6.5-hour arm; the truth was ~2. The
head of `prime-rel` averages 5,278 chars against the corpus mean of 1,761,
and `--limit` always samples the head. The engine's own ETA counts nodes,
so it read 152 → 180 → 198 minutes while the arm was 11% through its nodes
and 28.9% through its characters (`B-RATE-UNIT-1`).

Characters are not a constant rate either: attention is quadratic in
sequence length, so a slice averaging 5,278 chars measured 1.94M
chars/min while the whole corpus sustained ~720k (`B-QUADRATIC-DOCS-1`).

## Assert on the data, not on exit codes

Every check that caught something real was a data check:

- `total_chars = 227,851,132` — **exactly 100.00%** of the corpus, proving
  the rel arm splits rather than truncates
- `edges = 8,100,498` — exactly `edges.jsonl`'s line count
- `nomic-wholedoc` and `qwen-wholedoc` both score lexical **0.20479**,
  identical to five decimals, because BM25 does not see the embedding
  model — an unplanned confirmation that tenant isolation is clean. The
  same identity held again after indexing, since `ef_search` cannot touch
  a lexical arm
- a warm-cache arm scoring `0.3590479185937771`, byte-identical to its
  pre-cache figure
- `vss-control` reproducing `0.23057383129905376` after a rebuild, which
  also proved redstring PR #71's rewritten ORDER BY behaviour-preserving
  to seventeen significant figures

And one warning that was **stale**: `self_loops_dropped: 0` is the correct
answer for the current PRIME export, which contains no self-loops in any
of its 8,100,498 edges.

## Compare against a sibling arm's predictions

One run's stderr was piped into `tail -40` and lost. The failure that
matters — every `extract` call failing, so the agent returns retrieval
order and scores *identically to `hybrid`* — was still detectable from
disk: 3 of 250 queries had a top-20 order identical to hybrid's, and none
were empty.

Comparing against a sibling arm catches the defect by its **effect**. A
log line catches it only if the exception was raised, caught and logged as
expected. The first is stronger and costs nothing.

## A number in the report that nothing was reading

`llm_calls_per_query` has been in every report from the beginning. It is
`1.0` for a healthy rerank arm, because the agent makes exactly one
`extract` call per query.

On 2026-08-21 two rerank arms ran against a chat peer answering `502 Bad
Gateway` — one for every call, one for 8.2% of them. The agent caught
each failure, logged `rerank: extract failed`, and fell back to retrieval
order, which is what it is designed to do. The reports read
`llm_calls_per_query: 0.0` and `0.9179`. Both wrote plausible mrr values;
the fully-failed one scored 0.29188, close enough to `hybrid` to look
like a weak architecture and far enough from it to look real.

Nothing checked the field. It took comparing a control against a number
it should have reproduced, three explanations deep, to look at it.

`agent_warnings` is now recorded in every report and any agent warning
makes the run exit non-zero — after the report is written, because the
predictions cost half an hour and are still worth keeping while the
number is not worth quoting. The gate counts log records at WARNING from
`stark_bench.agents` rather than asking each agent for a tally: it needs
nothing from the `Agent` protocol and cannot drift from the warning it
counts, because it *is* the warning.

The test for it was itself weak on the first attempt — it asserted a
`SystemExit` appeared in the run path, which `if False: raise
SystemExit(...)` satisfies. Found by making exactly that edit and
watching the test stay green.

## The helper works, nobody calls it

Shipped twice in one session historically, guarded against twice more
since. Deleting `embedding_cache=` from the CLI call site leaves all 212
other tests green; only an AST check on the call site catches it.

It recurred while writing the provenance block for this very campaign: the
first draft wrapped its query in `except UndefinedTableError: return {}`,
and a typo in my own SQL — `s.idx_scan` against an alias named `i` —
raises exactly that. The run wrote an empty provenance block and reported
success, indistinguishable from an unindexed corpus. **A check that cannot
fail loudly is not a check.**

**Break the implementation on purpose and watch the suite go red.** Every
test added this campaign was verified that way, each against its own
specific defect.

# Part IV — Bugs found

Finding these is a legitimate output of this project — it is the first
serious external consumer of these code paths.

## In redstring

1. **The semantic ordering could never use an ANN index** (PR #71).
   `ORDER BY 1 - (embedding <=> $2)/2 DESC` is unservable by any pgvector
   opclass, so retrieval was a sequential scan over the tenant regardless
   of what indexes existed. B10k's three proposed routes all assume the
   ordering is already the distance form, so all three would have failed
   on arrival. Same PR: a NUL byte in a type filter raised from Postgres
   where the port promises it never raises, a divergence the compliance
   suite had been failing on.
2. **`BoundaryPreferenceChunker` degenerated to 1-character advances**
   (PR #64, merged). Found by looking at the data — "does any document
   justify 1,018 chunks averaging 147 characters?"
3. **`SlidingWindowChunker` emits one fully-redundant tail chunk** per
   document longer than the window whenever overlap > 0
   (`B-SLIDING-REDUNDANT-1`).
4. **`EmbeddingProvider` could not express task prefixes.** Design gap,
   closed by ADR 0043.

## In this harness

1. **`WholeDocumentChunker` ignored `max_chunk_size`.** The ingest
   engine's re-split exists so a character cap need not be guessed right;
   against a chunker that ignores the cap it halves four times, re-chunks
   identically, and raises the provider's error instead of the real one —
   a **guaranteed no-op for the chunker that most needs it**, since
   whole-document arms are exactly the ones feeding a context-limited
   server. Killed a 46-minute ingest.
2. **A reranker returning `{"scores": []}`** for most queries, silently
   falling through to retrieval order and therefore scoring identically to
   `hybrid`. Caused by stripping schema docstrings to save tokens — which
   removed the only text telling the model what to put in the array. It
   would have read as "titles are not enough", the exact hypothesis the
   arm was built to test.
3. **189 chunks silently deduplicated**, because chunk ids are
   content-addressed on `(source, text)` with no `start_char`, so sliding
   windows over repetitive text collapse on upsert.
4. **`seconds_total` quietly stopped being wall time** when the runner
   became concurrent.
5. **A rerank arm silently degrades to retrieval order** when its
   `extract` calls fail, scoring like a slightly-worse `hybrid` with a
   warning in a log nobody reads. Not a defect in the agent — the
   fallback and its warning are both deliberate — but a defect in what
   the harness did with them. Closed by `agent_warnings` and a non-zero
   exit.

**Every real bug in this project has been silent.** None raised an
exception at the point of failure; each surfaced because a number looked
wrong. The corollary: a zero, an empty, and a perfect score are the
results most in need of suspicion.

# Part V — Cost

| | |
|---|---|
| ingest, `prime-rel` whole-document | 61 min, 129,375 nodes, 100.00% of text |
| edge load, 8.1M relationships | ~28 min |
| `dense` scoring, indexed | 6–8 s |
| `lexical` / `hybrid` scoring | 1–4 min |

| HNSW index build | ~1 min per 130k rows at 1024 dims |

**The embedding cache** (`kg_embedding_cache`, keyed on
`(model, document_prefix, sha256(text))`) measured on `prime-mini`:

| case | wall | hit rate | speedup |
|---|---|---|---|
| cold | 293.8 s | 1.7% | — |
| same config again | **14.1 s** | 100% | **21x** |
| different chunker | 409.3 s | **44.7%** | ~1.45x |

The third row corrected an estimate that was too rosy — predicted from 86%
of `prime` being under 1,000 characters. **Hit rate is per chunk, not per
document**: a finer chunker emits 2.43 chunks/node against 1.20, and every
extra chunk is a guaranteed miss. Where it pays enormously is re-running
the *same* configuration.

# Part VI — What is worth doing next

1. **The `rerank40` control at `ef=800`** on `qwen-rel-whole`, which
   removes the basis confound from I.3. In flight.
2. **A reranker-model A/B.** `--chat-model` makes this one variable:
   `rerank40` on `qwen-rel-whole` under `gemma-4-26b-qat` against
   `qwen3.8-27b-64k-txt`. It says how much of the rerank result is
   architecture and how much is the served model — the first thing anyone
   will ask.
3. **`zero_shot` and `deep` on a live corpus**, which needs
   `--ingest-edges` on a qwen tenant first (I.4).
4. **A size sweep on the embedder.** `qwen3-embedding-0.6b` loses to
   ada-002 by 0.064 on the dense channel, which makes the hybrid result
   more interesting rather than less. `Qwen3-Embedding-4B` or `8B` would
   say whether the gap is the 0.6B or the family. New `embeddings:`
   identifier, new table by construction, full re-ingest.
5. **A search-engine arm.** Checked 2026-08-21: the STaRK leaderboard has
   **no** search-engine baseline and zero PRIME submissions from anyone,
   and Typesense publishes only throughput and latency, never relevance.
   The gap is real and the agent seam makes it a `Toolset`, not a fork.
   Two things to state up front: Typesense's fusion is `0.7*K + 0.3*S`
   over ranks, **not** RRF, so it is a different function rather than a
   retuning of ours; and its joins are query-time filters, not traversal,
   so the path to a competitive arm is denormalising the relations block
   the way `qwen-rel-whole` does.
6. **MAG**, the corpus-generalisation cell. ~849M chars, ~7h locally, and
   the place to re-check `ef_search` — II.5's rule says sweep on the
   largest corpus you have, and MAG is another order of magnitude up.
7. **A backend re-baseline on TEI or vLLM**, so numbers are for the model
   rather than for a Q8_0 GGUF served by a text-generation engine. Costs a
   re-ingest of every arm; belongs with the MAG work.
